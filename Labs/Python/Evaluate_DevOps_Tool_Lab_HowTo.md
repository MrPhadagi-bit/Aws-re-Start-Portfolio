# Evaluate a DevOps Tool: Lab Guide

> **Course:** DevOps Fundamentals  
> **Lab Duration:** 45 minutes  
> **Lab Type:** Group Research & Presentation  
> **Last Updated:** June 2026


## What You Will Do in This Lab

In this lab, you will work with a small group to research a DevOps tool and then present your findings to the class. Here is the big picture:

1. **Get into a group** with other students
2. **Choose one DevOps tool** from a provided list
3. **Spend 30 minutes researching** that tool using the internet
4. **Prepare a short presentation** (2-3 minutes)
5. **Present your findings** to the rest of the class

That is it. The entire lab takes about 45 minutes.

---

## Before You Start

### What You Need

| Item | Why You Need It |
|------|-----------------|
| Internet access | To look up information about your tool |
| A way to take notes | Pen and paper, a shared document, or a whiteboard |
| A way to present | Slides, a whiteboard, or just talking clearly |

### What You Should Already Know

You do not need to be an expert. You just need a basic idea of what DevOps is: a set of practices that combines software development and IT operations to shorten the development lifecycle and deliver software faster.

## Step-by-Step Instructions

Follow these steps in order.

---

### Part 1: Form Your Group

**What to do:**

1. Wait for your instructor to tell you to form groups, or divide yourselves into groups.
2. Each group should have about **5 people**. If there are not enough people for a full group of 5, that is okay. Try to keep groups roughly the same size.
3. Once your group is formed, pick a place to sit or work together.

**How to divide work within your group:**

Assign one person to each role below. This saves time and keeps everyone busy.

| Role | What This Person Does |
|------|----------------------|
| **Research Lead** | Decides what to search for and keeps the group on track |
| **Technical Analyst** | Focuses on finding out what the tool actually does and how it works |
| **Use Case Specialist** | Finds real examples of companies or teams using the tool |
| **Presenter** | Takes the notes and prepares to speak to the class |
| **Scribe** | Writes down all findings so nothing is forgotten |

> **Tip:** If your group has fewer than 5 people, combine roles. For example, one person can be both the Research Lead and the Scribe.

---

### Part 2: Pick a Tool

**What to do:**

1. Look at the list of tools below.
2. As a group, pick **one tool** you want to research.
3. If your instructor assigns a tool to your group, use that one instead.

#### The Tool List

**Monitoring Tools** (these watch your systems and alert you when something goes wrong):

| Tool | Think of It As... |
|------|-------------------|
| **Nagios** | A watchdog for your servers and networks |
| **WhatsUp Gold** | A map and health monitor for your entire network |
| **ManageEngine** | A full toolkit for managing IT operations |

**Build and Test Tools** (these automate building software and running tests):

| Tool | Think of It As... |
|------|-------------------|
| **AWS CodeDeploy** | A robot that pushes your code to Amazon servers automatically |
| **Jenkins** | A factory worker that builds, tests, and deploys your code |
| **Electric Cloud** | A supercharged build system for huge, complex projects |

> **Tip:** If your group cannot decide, pick the tool name you have heard before or the one that sounds most interesting.

---

### Part 3: Research the Tool

**What to do:**

You have **30 minutes** to find answers to three questions about your tool. Everyone in the group should participate in the research.

#### How to Spend Your 30 Minutes

```
Minutes 0-10   →  Answer Question 1: What does the tool do?
Minutes 10-20  →  Answer Question 2: Why is that important?
Minutes 20-30  →  Answer Question 3: What are specific use cases?
```

#### Where to Look for Information

Use these sources:

- The tool's official website (usually the most accurate)
- Wikipedia (good for a quick overview)
- YouTube tutorials or demo videos
- Tech blogs or review sites like G2 or Capterra

> **Tip:** Search for your tool name plus words like "overview," "features," "use case," or "tutorial."

---

### Part 4: Build Your Presentation

**What to do:**

After researching, spend a few minutes organizing your findings into a short presentation.

#### Presentation Format (2-3 Minutes)

Follow this structure:

| Part | What to Say | Time |
|------|-------------|------|
| **Introduction** | "We researched [Tool Name], a [Monitoring/Build & Test] tool." | 15 seconds |
| **What It Does** | Explain the tool's main job and key features in plain language. | 45 seconds |
| **Why It Matters** | Explain the problem it solves and why companies care. | 45 seconds |
| **Use Cases** | Give 1-2 real or realistic examples of the tool in action. | 45 seconds |
| **Wrap-Up** | One sentence summary. | 15 seconds |

#### What to Use for Visuals

You do not need fancy slides. You can use:

- A whiteboard or flip chart with bullet points
- A simple shared document projected on a screen
- No visuals at all, just clear talking

> **Tip:** Write down the key points on an index card or sticky note so the presenter does not forget anything.

---

### Part 5: Present to the Class

**What to do:**

1. When it is your group's turn, the presenter stands up and speaks.
2. Keep it to **2-3 minutes**. The instructor will time you.
3. After your presentation, the class or instructor may ask 1-2 quick questions.
4. While other groups present, listen actively. Think of one question you could ask them.

## How to Research Each Question

Here is exactly what to look for when answering each of the three research questions.

---

### Question 1: What does the tool do?

**What you are looking for:**

- The tool's main purpose
- Its most important features
- What kind of software or systems it works with
- Whether it is free/open-source or paid

**Example of a good answer:**

> "Jenkins is an open-source automation server. Its main job is to automatically build, test, and deploy software. It works by running 'pipelines' — step-by-step instructions written in code. It has thousands of plugins, so it can connect to tools like GitHub, Docker, and Slack. It runs on Windows, Linux, and Mac."

**Where to find this:**

- The tool's homepage
- The "Features" or "Product" page on its website
- Wikipedia's opening paragraph

---

### Question 2: Why is that an important need?

**What you are looking for:**

- What problem the tool solves
- What happens if you do not use the tool (manual work, errors, delays)
- Why businesses care about this problem (money, time, risk)

**Example of a good answer:**

> "Without Jenkins, developers have to manually build and test their code every time they make a change. This is slow, boring, and easy to mess up. If a bug gets through, it can break the live application and cost the company money. Jenkins automates this, so bugs are caught early and releases happen faster."

**Where to find this:**

- The "Why [Tool Name]?" or "Benefits" page on the website
- Case studies or customer stories
- Blog posts about DevOps challenges

---

### Question 3: Identify specific use cases for the tool.

**What you are looking for:**

- Real or realistic examples of the tool being used
- What kind of company or team uses it
- What specific task they use it for
- What result they got

**Example of a good answer:**

> "A large online store might use Nagios to monitor 500 servers. If a server's CPU goes above 90%, Nagios sends an email to the IT team immediately. This prevents the website from crashing during a sale. Another example: a hospital might use Nagios to make sure patient record systems are always online."

**Where to find this:**

- The "Customers," "Case Studies," or "Success Stories" page
- Search for "[Tool Name] use case" or "[Tool Name] example"
- Make up a realistic example if you cannot find a real one

## Tips for a Great Presentation

| Do This | Avoid This |
|---------|------------|
| Speak clearly and slowly | Rushing through your notes |
| Use one concrete example | Speaking only in generalities |
| Make eye contact with the class | Reading every word from a screen |
| Stay within the 2-3 minute limit | Going over time |
| Answer questions honestly | Making up information you are unsure about |
